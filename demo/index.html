Two identical assets. Two different numbers.

Before the case study, two things the room needs to hold: what the numbers are, and why they rule.

KPIs, Tier rates and quartiles

  • Plant data is aggregated into a few key performance indicators (KPIs): mechanical availability, utilization, maintenance cost as a share of asset value.
  • Beside them, the Tier 1 to Tier 4 process-safety event rates every operator reports under API RP 754.
  • And the peer benchmarks that place each plant in a quartile against the industry.
  • Together they let management judge an asset above the technical jargon.

Why KPIs, Tier rates and quartiles

  • Credit: lenders and rating agencies read unplanned downtime as EBITDA volatility, and EBITDA against debt moves the rating.
  • Insurance: the risk survey that sets the premium reviews process-safety indicators and maintenance practice alongside the plant.
  • Bonus: the lagging indicators, Tier 1 events and loss of containment, go in the annual report and the incentive plan.
  • Management quotas and targets follow from all three.

The pressure

Five screens, no reason. So the question goes down the line as a target.

What goes down

Asset B gets Asset A's numbers as next year's targets: availability 97 %, maintenance 2 % RAV, two turnaround days back. The three-year OE plan is attached. The site is asked to explain the gap it cannot see either.

Best case

Nothing moves. The site spends on planners, overtime and a consultant; the number stays where it was, because the cause is upstream of every metric on the scorecard. The spend is the cost of not knowing what the number rests on.

Worst case

The number moves. A test interval is stretched, a proof test slips past the turnaround, a setpoint goes up to quiet an alarm. Availability improves for two quarters. Asset B is lagging because it is carrying a safety constraint Asset A is not, and the pressure is now pushing against the constraint.

Two documents that live worlds apart.

Two things the room needs before the story: what a hazard study is, and what a change form is.

PHA · HAZOPthe study

  • A Process Hazard Analysis (PHA) is the systematic study an operations team undertakes to identify every credible hazard, for every part of a plant, and the safeguards that stand against it.
  • The result is a worksheet of hundreds of rows: the document the plant is allowed to run on.
  • OSHA mandates that they revalidate it, do it again, every five years.

MOCthe change

  • Management of Change (MOC) is the form that asks whether a change is safe: a new well tied in, a setpoint moved, a valve relocated.
  • The MOC process re-opens and reviews the PHA that directly covers the change. Nothing in the process mandates the re-opening of any other.
  • The change can reach other PHAs indirectly: a shared flare line, a transmitter two units borrow, a rate another study assumed. Any other PHA that quietly depends on the change is examined only if an engineer's intuition says to.

The problem: the change that was reviewed alone

A review of 1,633 accident reports put a management-of-change failure behind roughly one in eleven; the folklore figure of 80 % has no primary source. Flixborough is the textbook case: a temporary bypass, no change review, 28 dead. The mechanism is always the same shape.

Year 0 · PHAThe study is done. Every row is true of the plant as built.
Year 1Normal operation.
Year 2 · MOCA change is approved. Its own PHA is re-opened; the rows elsewhere that rested on the old basis are not.
Year 3The plant runs on a study that is no longer true.
Year 4Still running on it. Somewhere a safeguard is credited with stopping a scenario it no longer can. Nobody knows which.
Year 5 · RevalidationThe next mandated look. If the gap is caught, it is caught here. If a demand came first, it was caught by the plant.

The gap is not data. It is reasoning.

Everything Cathy searched for exists and is findable. What does not exist is anything that can say which of it the change touches.

Retrieving the PHA: solved

  • Document control finds the sheet by tag in seconds.
  • PHA software holds every worksheet, revision and recommendation, searchable.
  • MOC workflows route the form, collect the signatures, attach the study.
  • SIS lifecycle tools keep the SRS and the proof-test record.
  • Enterprise search puts all of it behind one box.

Deciding which PHA the MOC breaks: not solved

  • Which LOPA credits rest on the rate that just moved?
  • Which alarm response times are still true at the new pressure?
  • Which safeguards share a transmitter with the trip?
  • Which study on another unit assumed this one's old basis?
no tool answers these · the answer lives in an engineer's memory of a study done years ago
Until now.
